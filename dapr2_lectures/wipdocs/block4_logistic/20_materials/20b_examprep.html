Course mark FAQs

How your final course mark is calculated

The final course mark draws on three components:

Component Percentage of final mark
Weekly quizzes (best 14 of 18) 10%
Assessed group report 30%
Exam 60%


For example, imagine:

quiz_mark   <- 75
report_mark <- 52
exam_mark   <- 60

(quiz_mark * .10) + (report_mark * .30) + (exam_mark * .60)
[1] 59.1

this ^ would be your final mark, given those marks on each component

FAQs about marks, progression

What overall course grade do I need to pass DAPR2?

  • 40%.

If I fail the exam, can I still pass the course?

  • Yes, if your quiz and report marks are high enough. For example:
quiz_mark <- 65;  report_mark <- 65;  exam_mark <- 25

(quiz_mark * .10) + (report_mark * .30) + (exam_mark * .60)
[1] 41

If I fail the exam, can I still progress to Honours?

  • Yes, as long as your course mark is above 40% at first attempt (that is, the first time you sit the exam, unless you have exceptional circumstances).

What happens if I fail the course?

  • If a student fails the course and does not have exceptional circumstances, they are required to switch degree programme, since DAPR2 is a core course in the Psychology degree programme.
  • If a student does have exceptional circumstances, then they will be offered a reassessment/null sit, which will count as their first attempt. Check with your student adviser!

Strategy: Suggested approach for Section A

Section A (30 marks)

  • 15 multiple choice questions (MCQs)
  • 2 marks per MCQ
  • MCQs cover topics from across the course
  • Similar style to weekly quizzes: each MCQ gives you four possible responses to choose from


Suggested approach:

  • Aim to spend 15–30 minutes on the MCQs, about 1–2 minutes per question.
  • Answer all the questions that you know.
  • Leave questions you don’t know until later.
  • Keep track of the questions you haven’t done, so you can return to them at the end.
  • If you’re not sure about an answer, go with your gut instinct (implicit knowledge can be powerful!)
  • If you don’t feel any gut instincts, then just guess, because a 25% chance of guessing right is better than no response at all!

Strategy: Suggested approach for Section B

Section B (70 marks)

  • 8 short-answer questions, which each include several related sub-questions

  • Covering a range of topics, where some questions will involve:

    • short calculations
    • explaining statistical concepts
    • interpreting model output provided in the question


Suggested approach:

  • Aim to spend about 90 minutes on the short answer questions.
  • Questions are arranged from the highest number of marks to the lowest (i.e., Question 1 is worth the most marks, Question 8 is worth the fewest marks).
  • Skim over all eight questions first.
  • Prioritise questions that are worth more marks and questions that you know how to answer.
  • Read the question text carefully, so that your answers include all the relevant information.
  • Each question and sub-question will tell you how many marks it’s worth.
    • As a rough rule of thumb, one mark → one sentence.
    • So for a five-mark question, expect to write five/six sentences.

General tips for approaching the exam

  • No questions require you to write or memorise R code. The closest thing will be interpreting some familiar output (e.g., model summaries).

  • Split your time in the exam according to how many marks each section/question is worth.

  • Try to give an answer for every question.

    • Section A: Guess if you need to!
    • Section B: A brief answer is better than no answer.

  • You will not need every equation on the equation sheet, but some of them might help you out.

  • Remember: We are trying to give you marks! Show us what you can do :)

Getting prepared for the exam

Study strategies: Active recall and self-testing

  • Don’t just read over labs/re-watch lectures! Instead, practice retrieving information from memory, so that your brain is trained to give it to you when you need it.

  • Develop and test yourself with your own flash cards. (I designed the learning objectives in my lectures to be a good starting point for self-testing.)

  • Develop conceptual understanding using the Feynman technique: Teach or explain an idea as simply as you can. Wherever you’re not sure what to say or how to explain something, brush up on those areas and try again.

  • Practice for the MCQs using the Learn quizzes marked “REVISION”: you can take these as many times as you like.

  • Practice for the short answer questions by working through lab exercises with pen and paper, where possible, especially questions that ask you to interpret model estimates.
