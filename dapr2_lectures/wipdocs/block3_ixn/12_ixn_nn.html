Specifying the interaction model


In mathematical notation:

\[ \text{wellbeing} = \beta_0 + (\beta_1 \cdot \text{outdoor_time}) + (\beta_2 \cdot \text{mm_rain}) + (\beta_3 \cdot \text{outdoor_time} \cdot \text{mm_rain}) + \epsilon \]


In R, where both of these options have the same result:


lm(wellbeing ~ outdoor_time + mm_rain + outdoor_time:mm_rain, data = outdoors_rain)

lm(wellbeing ~ outdoor_time * mm_rain, data = outdoors_rain)


Does an interaction model need to contain each of the interacting predictors on their own, too?

Yes—otherwise we’d only see how one predictor’s effect depends on another predictor, with no idea how they behave individually.

  • Good: Y ~ A + B + A:B
  • Good: Y ~ A * B
  • Not good: Y ~ A:B

Interactions are symmetrical

A common example of symmetry: When a shape looks the same, even when it is mirrored.

Image from Wikimedia Commons


If we have an interaction between predictors A and B, then both “mirror images” are always true:

  • The association between A and the outcome is different for different values of B.
  • The association between B and the outcome is different for different values of A.

So we say that interactions are symmetrical.


In practice, usually we choose the interpretation that’s narratively simpler or makes more intuitive sense (like I secretly did last week!)

Simple slopes

Simple slopes

A simple slope is the slope of the association between one predictor and the outcome, at a specific value of another predictor.

I’ve been showing you simple slopes when interpreting interaction coefficients. For example:

  • the association of outdoor_time with wellbeing when …
    • mm_rain=0 is 5.33
    • mm_rain=1 is 5.33 – 0.03 = 5.30
    • mm_rain=2 is 5.33 – 0.03 – 0.03 = 5.27
    • …

^ each of those is a simple slope!


But now let’s see how to calculate simple slopes by hand for any predictor value.

What we need to know:

  • The model’s linear expression
  • The values of each \(\beta\) coefficient

Calculating simple slopes by hand (Example 1)


The model’s linear expression (where w stands for wellbeing):

\[ \text{w} = \beta_0 + (\beta_1 \cdot \text{outdoor_time}) + (\beta_2 \cdot \text{mm_rain}) + (\beta_3 \cdot \text{outdoor_time} \cdot \text{mm_rain}) + \epsilon \]

The model’s coefficients:

parameter beta estim
(Intercept) beta0 67.83
outdoor_time beta1 5.33
mm_rain beta2 -0.08
outdoor_time:mm_rain beta3 -0.03


Step 1: Substitute the estimated coefficients into the linear expression.

\[ \text{w} = 67.83 + (5.33 \cdot \text{outdoor_time}) + (-0.08 \cdot \text{mm_rain}) + (-0.03 \cdot \text{outdoor_time} \cdot \text{mm_rain}) + \epsilon \]

Calculating simple slopes by hand (Example 1)


Step 1: Substitute the estimated coefficients into the linear expression.

\[ \text{w} = 67.83 + (5.33 \cdot \text{outdoor_time}) + (-0.08 \cdot \text{mm_rain}) + (-0.03 \cdot \text{outdoor_time} \cdot \text{mm_rain}) + \epsilon \]


Step 2: Decide what specific value of mm_rain we want to find the slope over outdoor_time for. Substitute that value in for mm_rain.

Let’s start with mm_rain = 0.

\[ \text{w}_{\text{mm_rain} = 0} = 67.83 + (5.33 \cdot \text{outdoor_time}) + (-0.08 \cdot 0) + (-0.03 \cdot \text{outdoor_time} \cdot 0) + \epsilon \]


Step 3: Simplify this expression by working out the multiplication and addition step by step.

\[ \begin{align} \text{w}_{\text{mm_rain} = 0} &= 67.83 + (5.33 \cdot \text{outdoor_time}) + (-0.08 \cdot 0) + (-0.03 \cdot \text{outdoor_time} \cdot 0) + \epsilon \\ \text{w}_{\text{mm_rain} = 0} &= 67.83 + (5.33 \cdot \text{outdoor_time}) + \epsilon \\ \end{align} \]

So the line that associates outdoor_time with wellbeing, when mm_rain = 0, has an intercept of 67.83 and a slope of 5.33. 5.33 is the simple slope.

Calculating simple slopes by hand (Example 2)


Step 1: Substitute the estimated coefficients into the linear expression.

\[ \text{w} = 67.83 + (5.33 \cdot \text{outdoor_time}) + (-0.08 \cdot \text{mm_rain}) + (-0.03 \cdot \text{outdoor_time} \cdot \text{mm_rain}) + \epsilon \]


Step 2: Decide what specific value of mm_rain we want to find the slope over outdoor_time for. Substitute that value in for mm_rain.

Let’s do mm_rain = 100.

\[ \text{w}_{\text{mm_rain} = 100} = 67.83 + (5.33 \cdot \text{outdoor_time}) + (-0.08 \cdot 100) + (-0.03 \cdot \text{outdoor_time} \cdot 100) + \epsilon \]


Step 3: Simplify this expression by working out the multiplication and addition step by step.

Breakdown of steps on next slide.

Step 3 breakdown


The starting expression that we want to simplify:

\[ \text{w}_{\text{mm_rain} = 100} = 67.83 + (5.33 \cdot \text{outdoor_time}) + (-0.08 \cdot 100) + (-0.03 \cdot \text{outdoor_time} \cdot 100) + \epsilon \]

Multiply the 100s together with the numbers in the same brackets.

\[ \text{w}_{\text{mm_rain} = 100} = 67.83 + (5.33 \cdot \text{outdoor_time}) + (-8.00) + (-3.00 \cdot \text{outdoor_time}) + \epsilon \]

Add together the freestanding numbers (the numbers not being multiplied with outdoor_time).

\[ \begin{align} \text{w}_{\text{mm_rain} = 100} &= 67.83 - 8.00 + (5.33 \cdot \text{outdoor_time}) + (-3.00 \cdot \text{outdoor_time}) + \epsilon \\ &= 59.83 + (5.33 \cdot \text{outdoor_time}) + (-3.00 \cdot \text{outdoor_time}) + \epsilon \\ \end{align} \]

Add together the numbers that are multiplied with \(\text{outdoor_time}\). (In math terms, we’re factoring out the variable \(\text{outdoor_time}\) from both terms that contain it, then adding the constants together.)

\[ \begin{align} \text{w}_{\text{mm_rain} = 100} &= 59.83 + ((5.33 - 3.00) \cdot \text{outdoor_time}) + \epsilon \\ &= 59.83 + (2.33 \cdot \text{outdoor_time}) + \epsilon \\ \end{align} \]

So the line that associates outdoor_time with wellbeing, when mm_rain is set to 100, has an intercept of 59.83 and a slope of 2.33. 2.33 is the simple slope.

What’s mean-centering again?

To mean-centre a variable means to transform it so that the mean of the centered version is zero.

Specifically, this involves subtracting the mean of a variable from every observation of that variable.

Revisiting this week’s learning objectives


How do we specify an interaction between two numeric predictors?

  • Exactly the same as we would for two categorical predictors!
  • Mathematically: Interactions are always the product of the two interacting predictors.
  • In R: If we have two interacting predictors A and B, we can write A*B (which stands for the individual predictors and their interaction) or A:B (which stands for just the interaction).

What does it mean when we say “interactions are symmetrical”?

  • Imagine we have two interacting predictors, A and B.
  • We can see the symmetry of this interaction because both of these “mirrored” interpretations are true:
    • The association between A and the outcome is different for different values of B.
    • The association between B and the outcome is different for different values of A.

Revisiting this week’s learning objectives


What is a simple slope?

  • The slope of the association between one predictor and the outcome, at a specific value of another predictor.
  • If we have two interacting predictors, A and B, then we could look at (for example)
    • the simple slope of A, when B = 0,
    • or the simple slope of B, when A = 100,
    • or the simple slope of A, when B = –5,
    • or …

When we have an interaction between two numeric predictors, what values do we typically use to compute simple slopes?

  • We typically look at the simple slope of one predictor at three specific values of the other one: its mean, its mean + 1 SD, its mean – 1 SD.
  • If we have two interacting predictors, A and B, then we might look at the simple slope of A
    • when B is at its mean minus one SD (i.e., mean(B) - sd(B)),
    • when B is at its mean (i.e., mean(B)), and
    • when B is at its mean plus one SD (i.e., mean(B) + sd(B)).
